Put a brain scan next to the word “addiction” and the story seems to write itself: the phone is damaging our heads. Add another study in which control regions “deactivate” while people watch short videos. There it is—cause, mechanism, and consequence in one neat line.

The problem is that the line does not exist.

The two papers behind this note can be read together, but in a more interesting way. One observes what happens **during twelve minutes of short videos**. The other compares a small group with problematic smartphone use against controls. The first is about a temporary state; the second is about associated differences. Neither watches someone travel from one to the other. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="Abstract and §2.1–2.2" /> <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="Abstract and §2" />

<Callout kind="thesis" label="The central idea">
  Short-form scrolling appears to put part of the cognitive-control network into a lower-effort mode while we watch. People with problematic use also show structural and functional differences. That deserves attention, but it does not yet prove that scrolling caused those differences or damaged the brain.
</Callout>

## Two studies, two clocks

In 2026, Tiantian Hong and colleagues recruited 66 young adults and analyzed 56 after excluding excessive motion or poor-quality spectra. Their mean age was 23.3. Inside a scanner, each person watched two six-minute blocks of clips and could advance whenever they wanted. The authors called a video watched to completion “liked” and one skipped before halfway “disliked.” <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="§2.1–2.2" />

In 2020, Juliane Horvath and colleagues asked a different question. They compared 22 people above a threshold on the *Smartphone Addiction Inventory* with 26 controls, all aged 18 to 30. They measured gray-matter volume and brain activity at rest. They did not assign a population to heavier phone use and watch what changed; they took a cross-sectional snapshot of two groups that already differed. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="Abstract and §2.1–2.4" />

<NoteFigure id="short-video-brain-state-vs-trait/two-studies-two-clocks" />

That difference in clocks changes everything. An experiment can describe with some precision what coincides with consumption **at that moment**. A cross-sectional comparison can identify features associated with a group. Showing that repeated exposure to the first state produces the second feature requires time, before-and-after measures, and a strategy for ruling out other explanations.

## What happened while they watched

The 2026 experiment focused on two regions that often work together when a task requires monitoring conflict, deciding whether effort is worthwhile, and applying top-down control:

- the **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex**, or dACC, contributes to monitoring and effort evaluation;
- the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, or dlPFC, helps execute control.

During completed videos, both regions showed lower BOLD signal than during rest. They also dropped more than during discarded clips. Meanwhile, visual cortex activity increased in both viewing conditions. The brain did not “switch off.” It redistributed its work. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="§3.2 and Fig. 2" />

<NoteFigure id="short-video-brain-state-vs-trait/while-you-watch" />

“Lower activity” does not mean a region lost capacity, either. The authors explicitly say that the deactivation **should not be interpreted as a failure of cognitive-control capacity**. Their preferred account is an adaptive immersion mode: a passive, pleasant, low-conflict activity needs less frontal monitoring and can rely more on automatic processing. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="§4.1" />

That fits a familiar experience. When a clip is compelling, you do not have to solve an equation or resist an incompatible instruction. When it is boring, a decision appears: stay or skip? But the study did not include a control test after viewing. It can describe the mode during consumption; it does not show that people leave with less self-control.

## Glutamate was not “spent by TikTok”

The neurochemical part of the paper is novel and unusually easy to distort. Before the task, the researchers used magnetic resonance spectroscopy to estimate glutamate and GABA in the dACC. They then related those resting concentrations to the fMRI response.

People with higher baseline glutamate tended to show **less dACC suppression** during both kinds of video. There was also an association with dlPFC during discarded videos; the completed condition landed at `p = .060`. GABA did not show the same pattern in the control network. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="§3.3, Fig. 3, and tables 1–2" />

The accurate sentence is: “a neurochemical difference measured at rest was associated with how much the network deactivated.” It is not: “videos reduce, release, or deplete glutamate.” The experiment did not take a chemical before-and-after measurement.

The paper also reports that dACC and dlPFC time courses were more coordinated than baseline during both kinds of clip. The condition comparison deserves caution: the text prints `p = .009` for “liked vs. disliked,” then states a corrected threshold of `.00833`. The general coupling signal is clear; the small preference-specific increment is not a solid place to anchor a headline. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-neuroimage-2026-121722" locator="§3.4 and Fig. 4" />

## The other snapshot: problematic use

The 2020 paper found that the group classified with “smartphone addiction” had lower gray-matter volume in the left anterior insula and temporal regions, along with lower spontaneous activity in the right anterior cingulate cortex. Scale severity was negatively related to ACC volume and activity and to left orbitofrontal volume. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="Abstract and §3.2–3.4" />

Those results attract attention because the insula, ACC, and orbitofrontal cortex appear in work on salience, control, and addictive behavior. The authors interpret the pattern as initial evidence of neural correlates resembling those seen in other behavioral addictions. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="§4 Discussion" />

But resemblance does not mean “the same disease,” and a correlate is not a cause. The article itself explains that *smartphone addiction* was not a recognized diagnostic category and that a phone can be the medium for very different activities: messaging, social networks, games, or browsing. The group was defined through a scale, not a complete clinical evaluation. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="§1 Introduction and §2.1" />

There were only 48 people in total. The design was cross-sectional, lacked a formal clinical assessment of possible comorbidities, and included exploratory correlational analyses. The authors acknowledge that they cannot resolve causality or temporal sequence. <Cite source="doi-10-1016-j-addbeh-2020-106334" locator="§4.2 Limitations" />

A brain difference might increase vulnerability to problematic use. It might follow it. Both could share causes such as depression, impulsivity, stress, sleep, or social habits. With one snapshot, we do not know which arrow to draw.

## How to combine them without inventing a movie

The papers are not consecutive installments of one experiment. Nobody followed the 2026 participants for years to test whether acute deactivation became the structural differences reported in 2020. The 2020 study did not isolate short videos, either; it studied problematic smartphone use in general.

<NoteFigure id="short-video-brain-state-vs-trait/not-a-causal-line" />

The valid connection is conceptual:

1. Engaging content can move the control network into a temporary, lower-effort mode.
2. Some people develop patterns of use that feel compulsive or interfere with daily life.
3. Within that second group, researchers have observed differences in regions related to control and salience.
4. The transition between those levels remains open.

That does not make the studies irrelevant. It lets us ask a better question. Not “Does TikTok destroy the brain?” but “When does a useful and reversible immersion mode become a pattern that is hard to interrupt, for whom, and with what durable consequences?”

Answering that requires longitudinal designs, objective use measures, clinical assessments, larger samples, and behavioral outcomes outside the scanner. It also requires separating the platform, the content, and the pattern of use.

## The ladder that keeps panic in check

A simple way to read neuroimaging is to sort each sentence into three levels.

**Observed:** BOLD signal fell here during this task; these two groups differed on this measure; a scale correlated with this region.

**Plausible interpretation:** lower activity reflects automatic immersion; group differences relate to control and salience; some people may be more vulnerable.

**Not established:** the video caused a chemical change; scrolling reduced gray matter; every user is developing an addiction; a scan diagnoses damage.

<NoteFigure id="short-video-brain-state-vs-trait/evidence-ladder" />

<Callout kind="limit" label="The most important limit">
  A region appearing in two papers does not create a causal mechanism. “dACC” may be the repeated name; the measured unit, population, moment, and question are still different.
</Callout>

## My take: look at behavior before the image

<MyTake title="What I take from this">
  Short-form video is designed to remove friction: decide less, receive novelty quickly, continue. The 2026 experiment makes a state compatible with that experience visible. I do not need to call it damage to consider how that design might compete with my intentions when I am tired, bored, or looking for a five-minute break.
</MyTake>

If I want to know whether my use is becoming a problem, a hypothetical brain scan helps less than concrete questions: can I stop when I decide to? Is it displacing sleep, work, or relationships? Am I using it as intended, or repeatedly ending somewhere else? Does the pattern continue despite consequences?

Those questions do not turn this note into clinical guidance. They keep the focus on functioning and autonomy, where “problematic use” gains human meaning. A scanner can contribute mechanism and association; it cannot replace a person’s story.

<MyTake title="Conclusion">
  Yes, the two papers belong in one post. Together they show two edges of a map: the brain mode during immersion and the correlates of a problematic pattern. The territory between them is still blank. The honest reading is neither “nothing happens” nor “your phone rots your brain.” It is knowing exactly what we saw, what we suspect, and what remains to be shown.
</MyTake>

Four things to keep: deactivation is not damage; baseline glutamate is not spent glutamate; a group association is not causality; and a temporary state is not yet a destiny.
