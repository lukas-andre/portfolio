Imagine an economy that can produce more and more with less human work. The machines are good, costs fall, and their owners earn more. People who live on a paycheck have a reasonable question: how much of that abundance will reach them?

One possible answer is to share the gains. But that requires the people making the decisions to have reasons to share.

*Automation and Repression*, by Daron Acemoglu, A. Arda Gitmez, and Mehdi Shadmehr, examines one such reason: fear of social conflict. It asks what happens when capital owners find it **more attractive to suppress a revolt than to reduce workers' reasons for joining it**. The result is an unsettling feedback loop: automation can make repression more appealing, and repression can make room for further automation. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§1, pp. 2–3; §5.4, Proposition 6, pp. 19–22" />

This is a theoretical paper, dated June 5, 2026. It constructs a simplified economy to follow that loop. Its strength lies in bringing a decision hidden behind the promise of productivity into view: who receives the gains, and what they do when sharing becomes less attractive.

## Earning more while receiving a smaller share

Start by separating two things that are easy to conflate: the size of an economy and the share that goes to labor.

<Callout kind="evidence" label="Arithmetic example · illustrative numbers">
  Suppose an economy produces 100 and pays 60 in wages. Later, it produces 150 and pays 70. Workers receive more money, but their share falls from 60% to 46.7%. Capital income rises from 40 to 80. These are invented numbers to illustrate the distinction, not findings from the paper.
</Callout>

The distinction matters because automation can affect both production and distribution. In the model, moving tasks from labor to capital reduces labor's income share. Absolute wages also depend on how much the economy produces: they need not fall with every increase in automation. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§4, equations 14 and 17, Proposition 2 and Figure 1, pp. 9–12" />

The paper's society has two groups: some own capital; others supply labor. A group of workers can attempt a revolt that, if successful, expropriates the capitalists. Participation is costly, and each person is uncertain whether enough others will join to defeat the regime. The authors model that uncertainty and derive a relationship between labor income, the reward associated with success, and the probability of revolt. **What matters is what workers stand to lose relative to what they expect to gain.** <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§2–3, equation 2 and Proposition 1, pp. 6–8" />

This is where politics enters: a larger economy can distribute its gains in ways that make the order protecting them more fragile.

## The state's calculation

The next assumption is decisive. The state represents capital owners and maximizes their expected income. That is what a “capitalist state” means in this model; society's overall welfare is outside its objective.

Its calculation looks roughly like this:

**Income capital retains × probability that the regime survives.**

Automation can improve one side of that calculation while worsening the other. A prospect of enormous income is less appealing if a successful revolt takes it away. Each individual firm ignores its small contribution to that collective risk; the state takes it into account. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§4–5.1, Propositions 2–3, pp. 11–13" />

It can **regulate automation** to preserve labor income. It can **redistribute**, giving workers more to lose if they revolt. Or it can **repress**, making a successful revolt less likely.

A revealing paradox follows. When only the first instrument is available, this state chooses the level of automation that maximizes labor income. It does so because, in this particular game, that is the best way to protect capitalists' income. Supporting wages acts as political insurance. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.1, Proposition 3, p. 13" />

<NoteFigure id="automation-and-repression/political-feedback-loop" />

Access to the other instruments changes the calculation. The question becomes how much each costs, and how those costs change as labor's share declines.

## The assumption that drives the argument

Redistribution is funded by an income tax and reaches workers as a public good. The authors assume that only workers who stay out of the revolt benefit from it. This is a specific form of redistribution: the model gives it political value because giving up both wages and the public good makes rebellion less attractive.

As labor's share declines, the state prefers a higher tax rate. Further automation therefore comes with an additional bill for capital owners: funding more redistribution. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.3, Lemma 2 and Proposition 5, pp. 15–17" />

Repression works differently. It costs a **fixed fraction of output** and leaves a fixed probability of successful revolt. This is not a one-time expense: as output grows, the amount spent can grow too. What stays fixed is the proportion, and repression's effectiveness does not deteriorate with additional automation. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.2, equation 21, pp. 13–14" />

Capitalists gain another advantage. Since they pay for repression, its cost takes a smaller bite out of their income as they capture a larger share of output. The same fraction of the economy can represent a shrinking fraction of their returns.


That asymmetry drives the result. Redistribution requires a rising tax rate; repression's cost does not rise in the same way as labor's share falls. Force can become the more attractive option for those making this calculation. Once it is chosen, part of the political constraint on further automation falls away. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.2, Proposition 4, p. 14; §5.4, Proposition 6, pp. 19–22" />

The assumption deserves as much scrutiny as the conclusion. If controlling a society becomes more expensive or less effective as discontent grows, the comparison changes. Within the model itself, costlier repression, a greater chance that it fails, or more valuable public goods all favor redistribution. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.4, Proposition 6, pp. 21–22" />

## More profitable for capital, even with less output

One of the paper's most interesting results reaches beyond the usual discussion of jobs: **maximizing output and maximizing capital income can imply different levels of automation**.

In this economy, the decentralized market automates up to the point of maximum output. Capitalists, if they could coordinate and had no fear of revolt, would prefer to go further. Beyond that point, total output falls, but capital's share rises enough for its income to keep improving. They can prefer a slightly smaller economy of which they receive a larger share. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§4, Proposition 2 and Remark 2, pp. 11–12" />

Under repression, the chosen level goes further still because of how repression is financed. Under redistribution, it remains below the output maximum: preserving some labor income continues to help contain conflict. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§5.2–5.3, Propositions 4–5, pp. 14–17" />

<NoteFigure id="automation-and-repression/automation-ordering" />

This is a reason to read the word “efficiency” carefully. A decision can be profitable for the people controlling capital without raising total output. The authors explain that their main mechanism does not require automation beyond the output maximum, but allowing it exposes the difference between the two objectives.

## Prosperity does not lock in the bargain

Introducing capital accumulation lets the authors ask whether a political response that is attractive today will remain attractive tomorrow. More capital makes further automation worthwhile. That can shift the comparison in favor of repression.

The theorem distinguishes three paths. One stays with redistribution. Another starts with repression. A third begins with redistribution and switches once enough capital has accumulated. The paths depend on the threat of revolt relative to the costs of control, initial capital, and the model's other parameters. They also describe an economy whose regime survives and whose capital keeps accumulating. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§6.4, Theorem 1, pp. 28–30" />

<NoteFigure id="automation-and-repression/capital-threshold" />

The third path is the uncomfortable one: sharing the gains at the beginning does not guarantee that sharing will continue. Economic success can change the incentives that sustained the bargain. The state can even choose repression when redistribution would allow faster growth, because it compares incomes weighted by the probability of retaining them. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§6.4, Remark 7, p. 30" />

The authors explore two important objections. When firms can **create new tasks for workers**, the mechanism persists under that extension's assumptions. When society starts as a **democracy**, a condition emerges under which capitalists prefer to coordinate a coup rather than keep paying the taxes chosen by the majority. That conclusion requires a highly simplified democracy and an ability to overthrow it that the game takes as given. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§7.3, Propositions 10–11, pp. 35–36; §8, Proposition 12, pp. 37–38" />

## What lies between the model and a country

The paper does not estimate how many robots push a government toward repression or identify a real capital threshold. Its contribution is to show how decisions can connect under explicit assumptions. The authors close by calling for systematic evidence and richer political models. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§9, pp. 38–39" />

Three pieces matter especially when leaving that framework. The first is **who controls the state**: representing capital is the starting assumption here, whereas in a particular society we would need to explain how that influence is acquired and maintained. The second is **what repression actually costs**, including effectiveness, resistance, and subsequent consequences. The third is **what redistribution achieves**: reducing the immediate incentive to revolt is only one effect that might matter.

AI as a surveillance tool also belongs on that open research agenda. The authors suggest it could make repression cheaper, but leave that channel outside their model. The central argument already works through technology's effect on income distribution, even without better tools of control. <Cite source="mit-automation-repression-2026" locator="§6.4, Remark 8, pp. 30–31" />

## What stays with me

<MyTake title="My take">
  What troubles me is the fragility of a promise of inclusion that rests entirely on sharing being convenient for whoever controls the gains. If that convenience depends on how much they need our work or fear our discontent, it can change as technology changes.

  That leaves me looking at automation through three questions together: how much it allows us to produce, who acquires rights to that income, and what ability everyone else retains to contest its distribution. A productivity improvement does not settle the last two.
</MyTake>

The paper leaves a useful question for any promise of automated abundance: **what sustains the commitment to share it when sharing stops being the most profitable option?**

My answer starts here: we should not have to remain economically indispensable to retain our rights.
